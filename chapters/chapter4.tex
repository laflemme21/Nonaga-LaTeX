\chapter{Results, Evaluation and Discussion}
\label{chapter4}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the system across three dimensions: the
throughput gain achieved by the C/Cython bitboard engine over the legacy pure-Python
implementation, the effectiveness of the adversarial search optimisations described in
\cref{imp zobrist}, and the quality of the weight vector evolved by the Genetic
Algorithm. Each result is interpreted against the design decisions documented in
Chapters~2 and~3.

The Cython engine sustains 1.44--1.54 million NPS across depths~1--3, while the
Legacy engine achieves 16{,}000--24{,}000 estimated NPS, placing the measured
speedup in the range $64$--$88\times$. A key methodological limitation is that the Legacy
figures are estimated from evaluation-function call counts rather than measured by
exact hardware counters, as no instrumented node counter was present in the legacy
codebase at the time of measurement. This introduces systematic uncertainty: the estimate
is sensitive to alpha-beta cut behaviour and may undercount or overcount depending on how
pruning distributes evaluation calls across the tree. The speedup figures should therefore
be treated as order-of-magnitude characterisations rather than precise ratios. Nevertheless,
a factor of $64$--$88\times$ is sufficiently large that no plausible measurement error
would reduce it to a negligible margin; the performance gap between the two engines is
unambiguous.

The speedup is consistent with established benchmarking of Cython extensions against
pure-Python equivalents in compute-bound loops~\cite{behnel2011cython}, and with DOD
studies reporting order-of-magnitude cache-efficiency gains when eliminating
pointer-chasing data structures~\cite{wingqvist2022dod,arantes2025impact}.



\subsection{Legacy Engine Scaling Failure at Depth~3}


\cref{tab:sps_comparison} presents the Searches per second (SPS) which is the number of full iterative-deepening sequence completed per second and mean wall-clock time per complete iterative deepening depth-$d$ search for both engines. A critical empirical observation is that the Legacy
engine failed to complete a single depth-3 search within the
100-second timeout. This is consistent with the theoretical game-tree complexity:
at a branching factor of~440 (\cref{branching}), exhaustive enumeration to depth~3
produces approximately $440^3 \approx 85$ million leaf nodes, which at 20{,}000 estimated
NPS would require roughly 70 minutes. Even with alpha-beta enabled, Legacy Engine real-time play at this depth is
entirely infeasible.

The Cython engine, by contrast, completes a depth-1 search in under half a millisecond
and a depth-3 search in less than 13~seconds without the transposition table.
\cref{sec:tt_effectiveness} demonstrates how the transposition table reduces this
to under one second in steady-state play.

\subsection{Discussion}

The $64$--$88\times$ NPS improvement is the product of two compounding engineering decisions rather than a single change. The primary contribution is the bitboard representation described in
\cref{sec:core_engine_design}: replacing pointer-chasing Python objects with packed bit arrays processed by hardware bit-manipulation instructions yields the cache-efficiency gains characteristic of Data-Oriented Design~\cite{wingqvist2022dod,arantes2025impact,browne2014bitboards}. The second, equally significant contribution is the migration of every performance-critical loop in the pipeline, move generation, legality checking, position evaluation, and the minimax kernel, to compiled C via the Cython bridge described in \cref{sec:system_architecture}. This eliminates per-bytecode interpretation overhead and removes the GIL~\cite{beazley2010} as a throughput ceiling entirely. The two factors are not readily separable from the benchmark data alone, as no intermediate build retaining Python data structures but compiling only the loop bodies was produced; the measured speedup should therefore be understood as their combined effect.

\subsubsection{Alpha-Beta Pruning}
\label{sec:ab_effectiveness}


Alpha-beta pruning alone reduces the per-search node count from 82.3 million to 21.5
million, a factor of $3.8\times$. As established in \cref{chapter1},
the theoretical best-case reduction is $O(b^d) \to O(b^{d/2})$, which at $b = 440$ and
$d = 3$ predicts fewer than 10{,}000 nodes under perfect move ordering. The empirical
$3.8\times$ reduction falls substantially short of this ideal, attributable to Nonaga's
exceptionally high branching factor producing a broad, flat position landscape at
shallow depths: many moves receive similar evaluation scores, so the alpha-beta window
is poorly constrained and cutoffs are less frequent than in narrower games.

\subsubsection{Transposition Table Effectiveness}
\label{sec:tt_effectiveness}

The most significant performance contribution comes from the transposition table operating
in concert with iterative deepening, as described in \cref{sec:tt_integration}. After the
TT is populated by the shallower passes (depths~1 and~2), the hash-move heuristic places
the cached best move at the front of the child list for the depth-3 pass, substantially
improving alpha-beta cutoff rates. The result is a further reduction from 21.5 million
nodes (alpha-beta only) to approximately 1.15 million nodes per depth-3 search, a $19\times$ additional reduction attributable solely to the TT, and a total
reduction of ${\approx}72\times$ relative to brute force. The corresponding
wall-clock time falls from 49~seconds to approximately 0.73~seconds, transforming depth-3
search from a computationally intractable operation into one viable for real-time
interactive play.

A secondary effect is a slight decrease in raw NPS when the TT is enabled: 1.38~MNPS
(alpha-beta + TT) versus 1.50~MNPS (alpha-beta only). As noted in \cref{back tt},
TT probe and write operations introduce per-node memory-access overhead. However, the
reduction in total nodes visited far outweighs this per-node cost, yielding the
net $67\times$ wall-clock improvement.

The TT warm-up behaviour is also evident in the Final Engine Benchmark data
(\cref{sec:final_engine}): the first sample at depth~3 on the
\texttt{saved\_board.json} fixture evaluates 660{,}140 nodes and achieves 110{,}021~NPS
with a cold TT, whereas the four subsequent samples settle into the 50{,}000--60{,}000
node range at 8{,}000--10{,}000~NPS once the table is populated.

Several observations follow directly from \cref{tab:tt_stats} and \cref{fig:tt_bounds}:\\
\begin{itemize}
  \item First, the hit rate under warm conditions is above 90\% at all depths. This means that in steady-state
        play, every position the engine encounters has already been evaluated and cached; the
        minimax kernel reduces to a sequence of hash-table lookups with no subtree expansion,
        which explains the very low steady-state node counts observed in \cref{tab:version_bench}.\\

  \item Second, the cold-start hit rate degrades sharply with depth: 99.98\% at depth~1, falling
        to 91.8\% at depth~2 and 70.4\% at depth~3. This reflects the well-established
        iterative-deepening reuse property~\cite{slate1977chess}: the depth-1 pass fills the TT
        with shallow evaluations; the depth-2 pass finds these entries and extends them; and by the
        time the depth-3 pass begins, approximately 70\% of the positions it encounters are already
        cached. The remaining 29.6\% are genuinely novel positions at the frontier of the depth-3
        tree (\cref{fig:tt_bounds}).

  \item Third, at depth~3 the \texttt{EXACT} bound accounts for 97.3\% of all hits, with
        \texttt{LOWER} bounds comprising 2.4\% and \texttt{UPPER} bounds less than 0.01\%. The dominance of \texttt{EXACT} hits indicates that the TT
        mostly returns complete, definitive evaluations rather than partial window bounds, which
        is the strongest possible form of TT reuse as it permits an immediate return without any
        further search.\\

  \item Fourth, the
        hash-move cutoff rate (HMCR) of 63.9\% at depth~3 quantifies the move-ordering
        contribution of the TT directly: when the hash-move is placed first in the child list, it
        triggers an alpha-beta cutoff in nearly two out of three cases, which is the primary
        mechanism by which TT and alpha-beta synergise to produce the ${\approx}72\times$ total
        node reduction reported in \cref{sec:ab_effectiveness}.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discussion}

The search evaluation exposes an important architectural trade-off: raw NPS and real-game
decision latency are different objectives, and the implementation correctly prioritises the
latter. The combined ${\approx}72\times$ node reduction falls significantly below the
theoretical $b^{d/2}$ optimum, primarily because the evaluation function has limited
discriminative power at shallow depths, leaving many move orderings poorly ranked. As
noted in \cref{sec:tt_integration}, no killer or history heuristic was implemented; the sole ordering
mechanism is the TT hash-move. The literature reports that these additional heuristics
routinely yield a further 30--60\% node
reduction~\cite{Artificial_intelligence,brange2021evaluating}, suggesting considerable
headroom for future improvement.

\section{Genetic Algorithm Optimisation Results}
\label{sec:ga_results}

To validate the weight vectors produced by the GA and determine the effect of
generation budget on solution quality, a post-optimisation round-robin tournament
was conducted on the HPC. The best individual from every other generation of each
of four GA runs (20, 100, 200, and 500 generations) was entered as a
participant, yielding 414 agents in total. Every pair of agents played two games
(one as each player, to account for any first-mover advantage), producing 826
games per participant and a total of approximately 170{,}000 games across the
tournament. All runs shared identical GA parameters; the sole variable between
them was the generation budget.

\subsection{Generational Improvement Signal}
\label{sec:ga_improvement}

The central question the tournament was designed to answer is whether the GA
consistently produces better individuals as generation count increases. Figure~\ref{fig:spearman}
reports the Spearman rank correlation between generation number and tournament score
for each of the four runs. All four correlations are positive, which is consistent
with the GA rewarding more performant genes over time. However, none reach
statistical significance: the largest correlation observed is $\rho = 0.355$
for GA-20 ($p = 0.28$, $n = 11$), while the longer runs, which are more
statistically powered, produce correlations below $\rho = 0.09$. The directional consistency across all
four independent runs is notable, but the effect is too small to survive the
noise floor introduced by the GA configuration.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{pictures/GA/plot5_spearman.png}
  \caption{Spearman rank correlation between generation number and round-robin
    score for each GA run. All correlations are positive but non-significant
    ($p > 0.05$ in all cases), indicating that generation number is not a
    reliable predictor of individual quality under the current configuration.}
  \label{fig:spearman}
\end{figure}

This outcome is a direct consequence of the cumulative non-determinism in the
GA design. Roulette-wheel selection, stochastic $k$-opponent fitness evaluation,
and random initialisation each introduce variance independently; in combination,
they produce a fitness landscape that shifts from generation to generation.
The original intention was to compensate for this through scale, running
sufficiently large populations over sufficiently many generations so that genuine
fitness improvements would emerge above the noise. In practice, the available
compute budget was not sufficient to reach that regime, and stochastic effects
remained dominant throughout all runs.


\subsection{Overall Solution Quality}
\label{sec:ga_quality}

Despite the absence of a statistically significant generational improvement
signal, the GA produces individuals that are meaningfully better than random
parameter selection would yield. The tournament winner, drawn from GA-200 at
generation 44 with genome $[19, 9, 33, 31, 26, 18, 30, 14]$, achieved 609
points by winning 608 of its 826 games (73.7\%). GA-200 contributes 50\% of
the top-10 participants in the tournament despite representing only 24.4\% of
the pool, indicating that the run produced a disproportionate number of elite
individuals relative to its size. These results confirm that the GA is capable
of converging on high-quality weight configurations, even if the generational
trajectory towards those configurations is obscured by stochastic noise.


\section{Evaluation Summary}
\label{sec:ch4_summary}

The C/Cython bitboard engine sustains 1.44--1.54 million NPS in raw processing rate,
a $64$--$88\times$ improvement over the legacy pure-Python implementation
(\cref{sec:engine_migration}), validating the decision to implement a custom hexagonal
bitboard from first principles~\cite{browne2014bitboards}. Because both engines execute
the same algorithm at a given depth, this speedup in processing rate translates directly
to an equivalent reduction in decision latency: the legacy engine required more than
100 seconds per depth-3 search; the Cython engine reduces this to approximately
12~seconds even without any caching (\cref{sec:engine_migration}).

Alpha-beta pruning reduces the depth-3 node count by a factor of $3.8\times$ relative
to brute force (\cref{sec:ab_effectiveness}). The transposition table, operating
synergistically with iterative deepening, contributes a further $19\times$ reduction
in nodes visited per search through the hash-move mechanism (\cref{sec:tt_effectiveness}),
yielding a combined ${\approx}72\times$ total reduction. In practice, this converts a
49-second depth-3 decision into a sub-second one under warm-cache conditions
(\cref{sec:tt_effectiveness}), enabling real-time gameplay at depth~3.

Brute-force enumeration of the evaluation-function weight space is demonstrated to be
computationally infeasible at any realistic scope (\cref{sec:hpc_feasibility}), with even a
severely restricted domain requiring approximately 169~days on 46-core HPC hardware.
The post-optimisation round-robin tournament (\cref{sec:ga_results}) confirms that the GA
produces individuals of genuine quality: the tournament winner, from GA-200 at generation~44
with genome $[19, 9, 33, 31, 26, 18, 30, 14]$, achieved a win rate of 73.7\% across
826 games. A statistically significant generational improvement signal was not observed
in any of the four runs, which is attributed to the cumulative non-determinism of
roulette-wheel selection, stochastic $k$-opponent evaluation, and the limited compute
budget available relative to the scale required to dampen these effects.
